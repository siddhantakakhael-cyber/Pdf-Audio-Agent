Unit One. Introduction to A I, Intelligent Agents, and Problem Solving. First, what is Artificial Intelligence, and a little about its history. Imagine teaching a computer to think, to learn from examples, and to solve problems just like a smart human. That is A I. Your textbook highlights three main branches of A I in India. The first is Machine Learning, or M L. This means teaching computers by showing them lots of past examples. For instance, helping farmers in India predict how much rice will grow this season, based on past rainfall. The second is Natural Language Processing, or N L P. This means helping computers read, listen to, and speak human languages. For example, customer care chatbots that talk in Odia, Hindi, or English. The third is Computer Vision. This means giving computers eyes, using cameras. For example, smart traffic lights that see crowded roads and change their signals to clear traffic jams.

Next, Narrow A I versus General A I, and how industries use them. Narrow A I is an A I that is super good at only one specific job. Think of a chess computer, a face unlock camera, or a voice assistant. All the A I we use today is Narrow A I. General A I, also called A G I, is a future dream machine that can learn and do any mental task a human can do, from painting a picture to doing science experiments. As for industry impact, doctors use A I to spot diseases on X-rays, banks use it to stop credit card thieves, and factories use it to pack boxes quickly.

Now, intelligent agents, search algorithms, and constraint puzzles. An agent is any robot or program that senses the world around it and then takes action. A Simple Reflex Agent reacts right away, the way you pull your hand back from a hot stove. A Goal-Based or Utility-Based Agent plans ahead, to reach a goal in the happiest and safest way. When an A I looks for the shortest road on a map, Uninformed Search checks every street blindly, one by one. Breadth-First Search and Depth-First Search work like this. Informed Search, like the famous A Star Search, uses a smart hint. It is like walking toward a tall tower you can already see in the distance, so you reach the goal much faster. Finally, Constraint Satisfaction Problems, or C S Ps. This means solving a puzzle where you must follow strict rules, like filling numbers into a Sudoku puzzle, or coloring a map so that no two touching states share the same color.

Unit Two. Knowledge Representation and Reasoning. First, storing knowledge with Propositional Logic and First-Order Logic. Computers do not understand messy stories by default. So we store facts inside their memory as neat building blocks. This allows them to diagnose illnesses or play chess. Propositional Logic joins simple true-or-false sentences, called p and q, using words like NOT, AND, OR, and IF-THEN. For example, if it rains, then the ground gets wet. First-Order Logic, or F O L, adds rules about groups of things using two special symbols. The first is For All, as in, all humans need water. The second is There Exists, as in, there is at least one red apple in the basket.

Next, inference and theorem proving. This means using stored facts to figure out brand-new truths automatically. It is just like a detective putting two clues together to solve a mystery. Then, probabilistic reasoning and Bayesian networks. Sometimes we are not one hundred percent sure about a fact. For example, a sneeze might mean a cold, or it might just be dust. A Bayesian Network is a neat cause-and-effect chart that calculates the exact percentage chance of each possibility.

Unit Three. Natural Language Processing and Computer Vision. Let us start with text preprocessing and tokenization. Before an A I can read a sentence, it cleans the text and chops it into separate words, called tokens. For example, the sentence, the quick brown fox, is cut into four word pieces: the, quick, brown, fox. Syntax and parsing means checking the grammar to see which word is the noun, the verb, or the subject. Sentiment analysis means reading a movie review or a shopping review and deciding whether the writer feels happy, which is positive, angry, which is negative, or neutral. Language models and transformers are the super-smart modern A I brains, like the ones inside ChatGPT and Gemini. They pay attention to all the words in a paragraph at once, so they understand the full meaning.

Now the vision half. Image processing means cleaning up a blurry or dark photo and sharpening its outlines. Object detection means drawing neat boxes around every car, tree, and dog in a picture. Face recognition means measuring the distance between the eyes, the nose, and the smile, to unlock a phone for the right person. Image segmentation means coloring every single tiny dot, called a pixel, of an image, so the computer knows the exact shape of the road, the sky, and the people.

Unit Four. Robotics and Perception. First, robot kinematics versus dynamics, including forward and inverse kinematics. Kinematics studies the geometry of movement, that is, where a robot arm moves when its joints bend, without worrying about weight. Dynamics calculates how much muscle push, meaning force and torque, the motors need to lift a heavy weight. Forward kinematics asks the question, if I bend my robot's shoulder by thirty degrees and its elbow by forty-five degrees, where will its hand land? This is calculated by multiplying the joint matrices, T equals T one times T two, and so on, up to T n. Inverse kinematics asks the reverse question. There is a teacup on the table. What exact angles must the robot bend its shoulder and elbow to, in order to touch the cup?

Next, SLAM and path planning. SLAM stands for Simultaneous Localization and Mapping. Imagine walking into a dark, unfamiliar room with a torch. You draw a map of the furniture as you walk, and at the same time you keep track of where you are standing inside that map. That is SLAM. Path planning and navigation means choosing the shortest and safest walking line from the door to the desk, without bumping into any chairs.

Unit Five. Expert Systems. A rule-based expert system is a computer program that copies a human doctor's or a mechanic's knowledge, using two main parts. The first part is the Knowledge Base. This is a big rulebook filled with facts and IF-THEN rules. For example, if the car engine will not start, and the lights are dim, then the battery is weak. The second part is the Inference Engine. This is the thinking part, which matches your problem against the rulebook to give advice. It is easy to understand and transparent, but it gets confused if a problem does not fit its exact rules.

Next, fuzzy logic, and ontologies with semantic networks. Instead of only saying strict hot, which is one, or cold, which is zero, Fuzzy Logic understands middle values, like lukewarm, which is zero point five. This helps smart air conditioners adjust their cooling smoothly. Ontologies and semantic networks are web-like mind maps that connect related ideas. For example, showing that a sparrow is a type of bird, and that a bird has feathers.

Unit Six. A I for Game Playing. The Minimax algorithm is how a computer plays chess or tic-tac-toe. It draws a game tree of future moves. The computer, called the Maximizer, picks moves that give it the highest score, knowing that you, the Minimizer, will try to pick moves that give it the lowest score. It looks ahead, which is called recursion, comes back up the tree, which is called backtracking, and passes the scores upward, which is called score propagation. Alpha-Beta Pruning is a smart shortcut. It stops looking down terrible move branches, so the computer can think twice as fast. Monte Carlo Tree Search, or M C T S, is used when a game has too many possible moves to check them all. The computer quickly plays thousands of random practice games in its memory, and picks the move that wins most often.

Unit Seven. A I Ethics. Let us look at bias and fairness in A I systems. A I can accidentally become unfair, or biased, in three ways. The first is data bias. If a face camera is trained almost only on photos of people from one country, it may fail to recognize people from other countries. The second is algorithmic bias. This is a flaw in how the computer's scoring formula was written. The third is human bias. This happens when the person building the A I accidentally puts their own unfair habits into the system. Now, where does bias come from? It comes from unfair old records, which is historical data, from testing only city people while ignoring villages, which is sampling bias, or from messy and wrong labels, which is measurement bias.

Next, privacy, governance, and jobs. This means keeping people's private photos and messages safe, making strict safety laws for A I companies, and helping workers learn new computer skills as robots take over boring, repetitive tasks.

Unit Eight. Current Trends and Explainable A I. Old A I worked like a secret black box. It gave an answer without explaining why. Explainable A I, or X A I, shows its step-by-step work, just like showing your steps in a maths exam. This way doctors, judges, and customers can trust it, follow the law, and fix mistakes. Edge A I and the Internet of Things means running A I right inside your watch, your phone, or your smart fridge, without needing the internet. Quantum A I and A G I means using super-powerful quantum physics computers to solve giant puzzles in seconds, and working toward human-level General A I. Finally, the A I project lifecycle. This is the four-step recipe for building any A I. Step one, pick the problem. Step two, collect and clean the data, which is called preprocessing. Step three, train and test the A I, using toolkits like TensorFlow or PyTorch. And step four, launch it and maintain it, which is called deployment.

And now, Appendix One, which is very important for your exam. First, how to use ChatGPT. ChatGPT is like a friendly tutor inside your computer or phone. Students use it to explain tough chapters in simple words, to make short notes, to create practice quizzes, and to check grammar. Teachers use it to plan daily lessons and to make question papers. Office workers use it to write neat emails, to summarize long reports, and to save time.

Second, the Google Gemini blueprint. Gemini is Google's smart A I helper, with three special superpowers. Number one, a connected ecosystem. It works directly inside your Gmail, your Google Docs, and your Google Drive. Number two, real-time information. It checks live Google Search, so its answers are fresh and up to date. Number three, multimodal capability. It can read text, look at pictures, listen to voice, and read P D F files, all at the same time.

Third, comparing popular A I tools. Just as a carpenter uses a hammer for nails and a saw for wood, smart students pick the right A I tool for each job. Use ChatGPT for step-by-step explanations and writing, Gemini for live search and Google Docs tasks, and specialized A I tools for coding or drawing pictures.
