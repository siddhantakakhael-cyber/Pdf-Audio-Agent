Unit 1: Introduction to AI, Intelligent Agents & Problem Solving

 1.1 What is Artificial Intelligence (AI) & Its History: Imagine teaching a computer to think, learn
 from examples, and solve problems just like a smart human. That is AI! Your textbook highlights
 three main branches of AI in India:
 Machine Learning (ML): Teaching computers by showing them lots of past examples—like
 helping farmers in India predict how much rice will grow this season based on past rainfall.
 Natural Language Processing (NLP): Helping computers read, listen to, and speak human
 languages—like customer-care chatbots that talk in Odia, Hindi, or English.
 Computer Vision: Giving computers "eyes" using cameras—like smart traffic lights that see
 crowded roads and change signals to clear traffic jams.

 1.2 Narrow AI vs. General AI & 1.3 Industry Uses:
 Narrow AI: An AI that is super good at only one specific job—like a chess computer, a face-
 unlock camera, or a voice assistant. All AI we use today is Narrow AI.
 General AI (AGI): A future dream robot that can learn and do any mental task a human can do
 —from painting to doing science experiments.
 Industry Impact: Doctors use AI to spot diseases on X-rays, banks use it to stop credit-card
 thieves, and factories use it to pack boxes quickly.

 1.4 Intelligent Agents, 1.5 Search Algorithms & 1.6 Constraint Puzzles:
 Agent Architectures: An Agent is any robot or program that senses the world around it and
 takes action. A Simple Reflex Agent reacts right away (like pulling your hand back from a hot
 stove), while a Goal-Based or Utility-Based Agent plans ahead to reach a goal in the happiest,
 safest way.
 Uninformed vs. Informed Search: When an AI looks for the shortest road on a map,
 Uninformed Search checks every street blindly one by one (like Breadth-First or Depth-First
 Search). Informed Search (like the famous A* Search) uses a smart hint—like walking toward a
 tall tower you can see in the distance—to find the goal much faster.
 Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs): Solving a puzzle where you must follow strict rules
 —like filling numbers into a Sudoku puzzle or coloring a map so no two touching states share
 the same color.

Unit 2: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning (KRR)

 2.1 Storing Knowledge with Propositional & First-Order Logic:
 Knowledge Representation: Computers do not understand messy stories by default, so we
 store facts inside their memory as neat building blocks—allowing them to diagnose illnesses or
 play chess.
 Propositional Logic: Joins simple true-or-false sentences (p, q) using words like NOT (∼),
 AND (∧), OR (∨), and IF–THEN (→). For example, "If it rains (p), then the ground gets wet (q)."
 First-Order Logic (FOL): Adds rules about groups of things using two special symbols: For All
 (∀) ("All humans need water") and There Exists (∃) ("There is at least one red apple in the
 basket").

 2.2 Inference & Theorem Proving: Using stored facts to figure out brand-new truths automatically
 —just like a detective putting two clues together to solve a mystery.
 2.3 Probabilistic Reasoning & Bayesian Networks: Sometimes we are not 100% sure about a fact
 (for example, "A sneeze might mean a cold, or just dust"). A Bayesian Network is a neat cause-
 and-effect chart that calculates the exact percentage chance of each possibility.

Unit 3: Natural Language Processing (NLP) & Computer Vision

 3.1 Text Preprocessing & Tokenization: Before an AI can read a sentence, it cleans the text and
 chops it into separate words called Tokens. For example, the sentence "The quick brown fox"
 is cut into four word pieces: ["The", "quick", "brown", "fox"].
 3.2 Syntax & Parsing, 3.3 Sentiment Analysis & 3.4 Transformers:
 Syntax & Parsing: Checking grammar to see which word is the noun, verb, or subject.
 Sentiment Analysis: Reading a movie or shopping review and deciding whether the writer
 feels Happy (Positive), Angry (Negative), or Neutral.
 Language Models & Transformers: Super-smart modern AI brains (like the ones inside
 ChatGPT and Gemini) that pay attention to all words in a paragraph at once so they understand
 the full meaning.

 3.5 Image Processing, 3.6 Object Detection, 3.7 Face Recognition & 3.8 Segmentation:
 Image Processing: Cleaning up a blurry or dark photo and sharpening its outlines.
 Object Detection: Drawing neat boxes around every car, tree, and dog in a picture.
 Face Recognition: Measuring the distance between eyes, nose, and smile to unlock a phone
 for the right person.
 Image Segmentation: Coloring every single tiny dot (pixel) of an image so the computer knows
 the exact shape of the road, sky, and people.

Unit 4: Robotics and Perception

 4.1 Robot Kinematics vs. Dynamics (Forward & Inverse Kinematics):
 Kinematics vs. Dynamics: Kinematics studies the geometry of movement (where a robot arm
 moves when its joints bend), without worrying about weight. Dynamics calculates how much
 muscle push (force and torque) the motors need to lift a heavy weight.
 Forward Kinematics: Asking, "If I bend my robot's shoulder by 30 degrees and elbow by 45
 degrees, where will its hand land?" (calculated by multiplying joint matrices T = T1 T2 … Tn).

Inverse Kinematics: Asking the reverse question: "There is a teacup on the table—what exact
 angles must the robot bend its shoulder and elbow to touch the cup?"

 4.2 SLAM & 4.3 Path Planning:
 SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping): Imagine walking into a dark, unfamiliar room
 with a torch—you draw a map of the furniture as you walk AND keep track of where you are
 standing inside that map at the same time.
 Path Planning & Navigation: Choosing the shortest, safest walking line from the door to the
 desk without bumping into any chairs.

Unit 5: Expert Systems & Unit 6: AI for Game Playing

 5.1 Rule-Based Expert Systems: A computer program that copies a human doctor's or mechanic's
 knowledge using two main parts:
 Knowledge Base: A big rulebook filled with facts and "IF–THEN" rules (e.g., "IF the car engine
 won't start AND lights are dim, THEN the battery is weak").
 Inference Engine: The thinking part that matches your problem against the rulebook to give
 advice. It is easy to understand and transparent, but it gets confused if a problem does not fit
 its exact rules.

 5.2 Fuzzy Logic & 5.3 Ontologies / Semantic Networks:
 Fuzzy Logic: Instead of only saying strict "Hot (1)" or "Cold (0)", Fuzzy Logic understands
 middle values like "Lukewarm (0.5)"—helping smart air conditioners adjust cooling smoothly.
 Ontologies & Semantic Networks: Web-like mind maps that connect related ideas (such as
 showing that a Sparrow is a type of Bird, and a Bird has Feathers).

 6.1 Minimax Algorithm, 6.2 Alpha-Beta Pruning & 6.3 Monte Carlo Tree Search:
 Minimax Algorithm: How a computer plays chess or tic-tac-toe! It draws a Game Tree of
 future moves. The computer (Maximizer) picks moves that give it the highest score, knowing
 that you (Minimizer) will try to pick moves that give it the lowest score. It looks ahead
 (Recursion), comes back up the tree (Backtracking), and passes scores upward (Score
 Propagation).
 Alpha-Beta Pruning: A smart shortcut that stops looking down terrible move branches so the
 computer can think twice as fast.
 Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS): When a game has too many possible moves to check them
 all, the computer quickly plays thousands of random practice games in its memory and picks
 the move that wins most often.

Unit 7: AI Ethics & Unit 8: Current Trends, XAI & Appendix-I

 7.1 Bias and Fairness in AI Systems: AI can accidentally become unfair (biased) in three ways:
 Data Bias: If a face camera is trained almost only on photos of people from one country, it may
 fail to recognize people from other countries.
 Algorithmic Bias: A flaw in how the computer's scoring formula was written.
 Human Bias: When the person building the AI accidentally puts their own unfair habits into the
 system.
 Where Bias Comes From: Unfair old records (Historical Data), testing only city people while
 ignoring villages (Sampling Bias), or messy/wrong labels (Measurement Bias).

7.2 Privacy, 7.3 Governance & 7.4 Jobs: Keeping people's private photos and messages safe,
 making strict safety laws for AI companies, and helping workers learn new computer skills as
 robots take over boring repetitive tasks.
 8.1 Explainable AI (XAI): Old AI worked like a secret "black box"—it gave an answer without
 explaining why. Explainable AI (XAI) shows its step-by-step work (just like showing steps in a
 math exam!) so doctors, judges, and customers can trust it, follow the law, and fix mistakes.
 8.2 Edge AI, 8.3 Quantum AI, 8.4 AGI & 8.5–8.8 AI Project Lifecycle:
 Edge AI & IoT: Running AI right inside your watch, phone, or smart fridge without needing the
 internet.
 Quantum AI & AGI: Using super-powerful quantum physics computers to solve giant puzzles in
 seconds, and working toward human-level General AI.
 AI Project Lifecycle: The 4-step recipe for building any AI: (1) Pick the problem, (2) Collect and
 clean the data (Preprocessing), (3) Train and test the AI using toolkits like TensorFlow or
 PyTorch, and (4) Launch and maintain it (Deployment).

 Appendix-I AI Assistant Simple 6th-Standard Explanation

 1. How to Use ChatGPT ChatGPT is like a friendly tutor inside your computer or phone:
 • Students use it to explain tough chapters in simple words, make short
 notes, create practice quizzes, and check grammar.
 • Teachers use it to plan daily lessons and make question papers.
 • Office Workers use it to write neat emails, summarize long reports, and
 save time.

 2. Google Gemini Gemini is Google's smart AI helper with three special superpowers:
 Blueprint 1. Connected Ecosystem: It works directly inside your Gmail, Google
 Docs, and Google Drive.
 2. Real-Time Information: It checks live Google Search so its answers are
 fresh and up to date.
 3. Multimodal Capability: It can read text, look at pictures, listen to voice,
 and read PDF files all at the same time.

 3. Comparing Popular AI Just as a carpenter uses a hammer for nails and a saw for wood, smart
 Tools students pick the right AI tool for each job—using ChatGPT for step-by-
 step explanations and writing, Gemini for live search and Google Docs
 tasks, and specialized AI tools for coding or drawing pictures.
